Q1 Real GDP per Capita

Question 1 asks about Japan's real GDP per capita. Using the IMF DataMapper, Japan's real GDP per capita is $35,700. The United States, by comparison, is $94,430 — about two and a half times higher. 〔pause〕

Real GDP per capita is calculated by taking a country's real GDP — which is nominal GDP adjusted for inflation — and dividing it by the total population. Adjusting for inflation is important because it removes the distortion of rising prices and shows true economic output.

What this implies about Japan is that it is a wealthy, high-income country — well above the world average of about fifteen thousand dollars — but it is significantly behind the United States. The gap reflects the U.S.'s larger workforce, more flexible labor markets, dominant services sector, and higher productivity. Japan's aging and shrinking population limits how fast it can grow output per person going forward.

Q2 Main Export Good

Japan's main export good is motor vehicles — cars, trucks, and automotive parts. Japan exports over $100 billion worth of vehicles per year. 〔pause〕

The macroeconomic mechanism that drives this is comparative advantage. A country has a comparative advantage when it can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than other countries. Japan specializes in capital-intensive precision manufacturing because it has a massive stock of industrial capital — advanced robotics, automated assembly lines, and decades of accumulated engineering expertise. Producing vehicles is relatively cheaper for Japan in terms of what it gives up compared to other countries.

One additional reason is Japan's culture of kaizen, which means continuous improvement. Japanese automakers invest heavily in robotics and automation, which gives them a quality and efficiency advantage that is very hard for other countries to copy quickly.

Q3 Capital Accumulation & the Production Function

For Question 3, I need to first explain the production function and then apply it to Japan. 〔pause〕

The production function shows the relationship between capital per worker on the horizontal axis and output per worker on the vertical axis. The curve rises steeply at first, meaning that when a country has very little capital, adding more produces big gains in output. But as capital increases, the curve flattens. This is called diminishing marginal returns to capital — each additional machine or factory adds a little less to output than the previous one.

Now applying this to Japan: with a real GDP per capita of $35,700, Japan ranks approximately 32nd out of 190 countries. This means Japan has a very large capital stock per worker and sits on the flat, right side of the production function curve. Because of diminishing marginal returns, adding more capital would boost Japan's output only slightly. What Japan really needs to grow is not more capital, but upward shifts in the production function — meaning better technology, innovation, and total factor productivity.

Q4 Institutional Quality

Question 4 is about institutional quality. Using the same production function from Question 3 — when institutional quality improves, like stronger rule of law, secure property rights, or a more independent court system — it shifts the entire production function curve upward. That means for any level of capital, the country produces more output, because institutions allow capital to be used more efficiently. 〔pause〕

According to the Liberal Political Institutions Index on Our World in Data, Japan scores approximately 0.73 out of 1.0. That places Japan well above the global average of about 0.35 and in the same range as other advanced democracies like France and Canada. Japan has a stable parliamentary democracy, an independent judiciary, strong property rights, and low corruption.

For long-run growth, this is a positive signal. Investors — both domestic and foreign — know their contracts will be honored and their property will be protected, which encourages continued investment and R&D. The main risks for Japan are not institutional — they are demographic and structural.

Q5 Human Capital & HDI

Question 5 is about human capital. Human capital is the knowledge, skills, and health that workers carry with them. It matters for long-run growth because more educated, healthier workers are more productive. More importantly, human capital drives innovation and technological progress, which actually shifts the production function upward rather than just moving along it. This is why countries that invest in education and healthcare tend to see sustained long-run growth. 〔pause〕

Japan's Human Development Index score is 0.920, which ranks Japan 24th in the world and places it in the Very High Human Development category. Japan has a life expectancy of about 84 years, an average of over 13 years of schooling, and a highly skilled workforce, especially in manufacturing, robotics, and technology.

This high human capital base is a key driver of Japan's strength in advanced industries. However, the challenge going forward is that Japan's population is aging fast. As older workers retire, maintaining and growing the human capital stock becomes harder, making immigration policy and female workforce participation increasingly critical for Japan's future growth.

Q6 Monetary Policy

Question 6 is about monetary policy. The Federal Reserve has three goals: maximum employment, stable prices — targeting 2 percent inflation — and moderate long-term interest rates. The two main goals are maximum employment and stable prices. 〔pause〕

When the Fed raises interest rates, it affects both main goals. For stable prices: higher rates make borrowing more expensive, so consumers and businesses spend less, demand falls, and inflation cools toward 2 percent. For employment: because businesses invest less and spend less, some may cut hiring, and unemployment can rise slightly.

Applying this to Japan: unemployment is 2.5 percent and inflation is 2.7 percent, slightly above the 2 percent target. If I were the central bank, I would raise interest rates — inflation is above target, unemployment is extremely low, and the economy can handle a modest tightening. 〔pause〕

The Bank of Japan acted consistently with this reasoning. After holding rates negative for years, the BOJ raised rates to zero in March 2024, then to 0.25 percent in July 2024, and to 0.5 percent in January 2025 — its first real rate-hiking cycle in decades. This matches the logic exactly.

Q7 Fiscal Policy

Question 7 is about fiscal policy. The best practice is to act counter-cyclically. During a boom — when the economy is above potential — the government should be contractionary: spend less and raise taxes to cool the economy and build up a surplus. During a bust — when the economy is below potential — the government should be expansionary: increase spending and cut taxes to stimulate demand and support jobs. 〔pause〕

Japan's government spending is approximately 38 to 40 percent of GDP, and Japan runs budget deficits of about 5 to 6 percent of GDP per year. Japan's output gap is currently close to zero — meaning the economy is at or near its potential.

Does Japan follow best practice? Not strictly. With the economy at potential, best practice says to pull back spending and move toward balance. But Japan continues to run large deficits. Part of this is unavoidable — Japan's aging population creates rising automatic spending on pensions and healthcare that cannot easily be cut. But it does mean Japan's fiscal policy is not following the counter-cyclical best practice that theory recommends.

Q8 Government Debt

Question 8 is about government debt. The debt-to-GDP ratio is calculated by dividing total government debt by GDP and multiplying by 100. It tells you how large the debt is relative to the size of the economy. 〔pause〕

Japan's debt-to-GDP ratio is approximately 255 percent — the highest of any major advanced economy in the world. For comparison, the U.S. is around 124 percent.

There is one big reason not to panic: about 90 percent of Japan's debt is held domestically — by Japanese banks, pension funds, and the Bank of Japan itself. Japan is essentially borrowing from its own citizens, which means there is very little risk of a foreign debt crisis or capital flight. Japan also borrows in yen, a currency it controls.

But there is also a serious reason to be worried: Japan's population is aging rapidly. Fewer workers means less tax revenue, and rising numbers of retirees means higher pension and healthcare spending. This combination makes it very hard to stabilize or reduce the debt over time, and it is a genuine long-term sustainability concern.

Q9 Unemployment

Question 9 is about unemployment. The natural rate of unemployment is the unemployment rate when the economy is at full employment — with no demand-driven, or cyclical, unemployment. It only includes frictional unemployment — workers between jobs — and structural unemployment — a mismatch between skills and available jobs. For the United States, the natural rate is estimated at approximately 4 percent. 〔pause〕

Japan's current unemployment rate is 2.5 percent. Compared to the assumed U.S. natural rate of 4 percent, Japan is 1.5 percentage points lower. This means Japan's labor market is running even tighter than what would be called full employment under the U.S. benchmark.

Since Japan is below the natural rate, cyclical unemployment cannot explain the difference. The explanation is that Japan has exceptionally low frictional and structural unemployment. Japan's "lifetime employment" culture — where major employers have historically avoided layoffs and workers tend to stay with one company for their entire career — dramatically reduces frictional unemployment. Strong skills alignment in Japan's manufacturing and services economy keeps structural unemployment very low as well.

Q10 Most Interesting Historical Phase

For Question 10, the historical phase I find most interesting is Japan's Post-World War II Economic Miracle — roughly from 1945 to 1973. 〔pause〕

In 1945, Japan was completely destroyed. Its cities, factories, and infrastructure had been bombed, and the country was facing starvation. Yet within 25 years, Japan had become the second-largest economy in the world, growing at nearly 10 percent per year — one of the fastest sustained growth rates in modern history.

This directly connects to the macroeconomic concept of capital accumulation from the production function. The war had wiped out Japan's capital stock, placing it at the far-left, steep portion of the production function curve where the marginal return to each new unit of capital is enormous. Japan's households saved 20 to 30 percent of their income, which funded massive investment in factories, machinery, and infrastructure. Each new unit of capital generated huge gains in output, just as the theory predicts.

As Japan's capital stock rebuilt and grew, it moved to the right along the curve, marginal returns declined, and growth slowed — again, exactly what the theory predicts. The miracle is basically a real-world proof of the production function in action. It is also why I think Japan is such a fascinating country to study — it has lived through both the dramatic steep phase and the flatter, mature phase of the production function within living memory.
